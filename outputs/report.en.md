# How would the tax plans change take-home pay? — Tedori Lab report (November 2026 edition)

## The main finding

**Under today's rules (2026 rules, before any plan), if a part-time spouse who works under 20 hours a week and is not in their employer's health insurance earns ¥1,300,000 a year instead of ¥1,290,000, the household takes home ¥258,226 less a year.** The spouse stops being a dependant, so National Pension (¥215,040 a year) and National Health Insurance (¥101,786 a year) must be paid; taxes fall by ¥48,600 in all. Take-home pay gets back to where it was when the spouse earns ¥1,640,000 (worked out in ¥10,000 steps). Example household H4: Shinjuku, Tokyo; one partner is an employee on ¥5,000,000 a year; no children.

- (Approximation) In a couple, we assume the partner with the higher income pays the spouse's National Pension premiums and deducts them from their own income (by law either partner may).
- From fiscal 2026, whether someone can be a dependant is judged from the yearly pay expected under their work contract. Going over the line now and then, for example through overtime that isn't in the contract, may not end dependant status. Here we work out what happens when the contracted pay goes up.
- A spouse who works 20+ hours a week at a company with at least 51 employees joins that company's insurance, so does not face this wall. The all-household estimate assumes every wage earner works like that.

Under the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules), the one plan that can be calculated now, take-home pay at the same point falls by ¥217,926 a year (¥258,226 under 2026 rules), and gets back to the same level at ¥1,600,000 (worked out in ¥10,000 steps). Until the income walls are removed, the government's plans include a temporary top-up (就業促進特例加算) for people who start paying company insurance premiums and whose earned income is below the level where the take-home pay lost at the wall is regained. Its amount hasn't been decided, and it is unclear whether someone like H4's spouse, who moves into National Health Insurance and National Pension, would qualify. From fiscal 2029, married people whose spouse's income is above a set level (not yet decided) also don't qualify for the payment. So it can't be calculated.

## About this report

The government, a trade union federation and a private research institute have put forward plans that change how working people's tax, social insurance and benefits work. Each plan states its own aims, such as easing the burden on people on low or middle incomes (see each plan below). Under the one plan that can be calculated now, take-home pay goes up for some households and down for others. One form these plans take is a "refundable tax credit" (給付付き税額控除): it cuts your income tax, and if the cut is bigger than the tax you owe, you get the rest in cash. In the government's plan, municipalities pay cash according to income, and the government calls it a "first step" towards a full refundable tax credit. Tedori Lab works out, with the same households, data and method, how 4 of these plans would change take-home pay (what is left after tax and insurance, plus child allowance and the single-parent allowance) for example households and for every household in Japan. Each plan is compared with "2026 rules": the rules actually in force in 2026, before any of these plans. We assume nobody changes how much they work.

Every number in this report is produced automatically from the same output files as the website. This report does not support any plan, party or politician.

## The 4 plans compared

- **Government plan (FY2027–28 bridge: 1% tax on food + payment)**: In fiscal 2027–28, consumption tax on food falls from 8% to 1%, and an income-based payment to people in work (the worker burden-relief payment) starts early, with conditions on earned income, total income and the tax and insurance you pay. The payment amounts and limits haven't been decided.
- **Government plan (full version, from FY2029)**: From fiscal 2029, the full version of a payment to people in work on low or middle incomes, once a year, based on the previous year's income and the number of children. There are conditions on earned income, total income, the tax and insurance you pay, and your spouse's income. The amounts and limits haven't been decided.
- **Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules)**: Replaces the income tax basic deduction with a fixed tax credit. Any part of the credit bigger than your income tax is paid in cash, up to the social insurance you paid on your work (people who pay none on work, such as those living on a pension, get no cash).
- **Rengo (trade union confederation) plan**: Takes half of lower-paid employees' insurance payments off their income tax (any extra paid in cash), and gives people on low incomes cash for consumption tax (while abolishing the reduced tax rate on food). The amounts for the second part haven't been set out yet.

**Right now only the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules) can be calculated.** The other 3 plans can't be calculated yet, for these reasons:

- Government plan (FY2027–28 bridge: 1% tax on food + payment): the payment amounts and income limits haven't been set out yet (under the bill, they will be set later by Cabinet order); we don't yet have food spending figures for the example households H1–H8.
- Government plan (full version, from FY2029): the payment amounts and income limits haven't been set out yet (under the bill, they will be set later by Cabinet order).
- Rengo (trade union confederation) plan: the payment amounts and income limits for the consumption tax refund haven't been set out yet (Rengo's plan gives only the approach); we don't yet have food spending figures for the example households H1–H8.

## Example households: Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules)

| Household | Who they are | Take-home pay under 2026 rules (yen a year) | Change under the plan (yen a year) |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Single part-time employee (¥1.25m a year) | 1,061,652 | +35,700 |
| H2 | University student with a part-time job, parents' dependant | 1,000,000 | ±0 |
| H3 | Dual-income employee couple, two children (2 and 8) | 6,248,008 | −27,500 |
| H4 | Single-earner employee, spouse working part-time | 4,961,316 | −23,900 |
| H5 | Single parent employee, one child (10) | 2,651,836 | +3,700 |
| H6 | Pensioner couple, both 72 | 2,611,921 | ±0 |
| H7 | Self-employed (freelancer), single | 2,170,281 | −25,200 |
| H8 | High-income dual-earner couple, one child (5) | 12,009,068 | −157,300 |

Of the 8 example households, 2 take home more, 4 take home less and 2 see no change. The largest gain is for H1 (+35,700 yen a year) and the largest loss is for H8 (−157,300 yen a year).

Daiwa wrote its plan for 2025 tax rules and set the credit at ¥56,500, so that an employee could earn up to ¥1.78m a year before paying income tax. Using the same formula with 2026 tax rules gives a credit of ¥52,000. Under 2026 rules an employee can already earn ¥1.78m a year before paying income tax, even without this plan. So the results here differ from the numbers Daiwa published. Under 2026 rules the basic deduction that is removed is larger and the credit is smaller, and both push take-home pay below Daiwa's figures.

## Every household in Japan: Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules) (estimate)

We lined up every household from lowest to highest yearly income and cut the line into ten groups of the same size. (Approximation) All of the following are rough estimates.

- (Approximation) In groups 1–4, take-home pay goes up on average (by the most in group 1: ¥9,257 a year). But within these groups, take-home goes up for 12–21% of households, and 79–86% see no change. In group 4, 7% of households are worse off.
- (Approximation) In groups 5–10, take-home pay goes down on average (by the most in group 10: ¥101,548 a year).
- (Approximation) Overall, income tax rises by about ¥1.5 trillion a year and about ¥0.4 trillion is paid out in cash (the part of the credit bigger than income tax), so the government takes in about ¥1.1 trillion more a year (a rough estimate that assumes nobody changes how they work).
- Daiwa's own estimate is about ¥0.7 trillion more income tax and about ¥0.7 trillion paid in cash: about zero overall (fiscally neutral). Daiwa used 2025 tax rules and a ¥56,500 credit; this site uses 2026 rules and ¥52,000, so the two can't be compared directly.

## Limits

- We assume nobody changes how much they work. Some people would change their hours when the rules change; that is not included.
- The example households live in Shinjuku, Tokyo. Outside Shinjuku, parts of National Health Insurance and resident tax (set by each municipality) are different, and outside Tokyo so is the health insurance rate for employees (協会けんぽ, set by prefecture).
- The all-household numbers are rough estimates from made-up households matched to the averages in a 2024 government survey. Compared with official figures, the household count is 2.8% lower. Total income tax is 21.6% lower and total resident tax 5.0% lower than the tax that survey itself estimates from reported incomes (not tax actually collected; income tax is lower because of recent tax cuts (a larger basic deduction), too few high earners in the estimate and taxing households' average incomes, among other things, which we haven't measured separately). The estimate has too many low-paid workers and too few high earners compared with official counts, so the cash paid out and the extra income tax may be off. Changing how the made-up households are built can move the national totals a lot, especially the cash paid out (see the Checks page).
- The plans that can't be calculated yet will be calculated once the amounts they need are decided and published (the reasons for each are listed above).

## All approximations

Behind every number:

- (Approximation) Each child's age stays the same for the whole year.
- (Approximation) The single-parent allowance (児童扶養手当) uses the amounts from April 2026 for all 12 months, judged on the same year's income (in fact, the previous year's income sets the amount from November to the next October). We assume the mother receives it and gets no child support payments.
- (Approximation) In a couple, the partner with the higher income claims the deductions for the spouse and children, and pays and deducts the other partner's National Pension contributions. By law either partner may do this.
- (Approximation) Long-term care premiums for people aged 65 and over use Shinjuku's fiscal 2026 table, applied to the same year's income and resident-tax status (in fact, the previous year's decide the band). Shinjuku's one-year adjustment for fiscal 2026, which works out salary income with the old employment-income deduction, is not included.
- (Approximation) We round the employee's share of health insurance and of the child and childcare support levy separately to the yen. A government Q&A (February 2026) said to round them once, together, but it is no longer published. The difference is at most ¥1 a month.
- (Approximation) From October 2026, the only people working 20+ hours a week for under ¥88,000 a month are workers with a minimum-wage exception (at every regional minimum wage, 20 hours a week pays more than ¥88,000 a month). They join company insurance only if they ask; we assume they don't.
- (Approximation) Whether a student may put off National Pension payments (the student deferral) is judged on the same year's income (in fact, the previous year's).
- (Approximation) Health and long-term care insurance rates change from March 2026 and employment insurance from April; the child and childcare support levy is new from April (the employee pension rate, 18.3%, does not change). We use the new rates and the levy for all 12 months of 2026. National Pension uses the fiscal 2026 monthly amount for every month. National Health Insurance uses Shinjuku's fiscal 2026 rates, because the fiscal 2027 rates (which would apply to 2026 income) aren't published yet. Pay is the same every month, with no bonus.

Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules):

- (Approximation) Income tax has a 2.1% reconstruction surtax added on top. Daiwa doesn't say whether the credit is subtracted before or after that surtax. We subtract the credit first and then add the 2.1%, which is how existing tax credits work.
- (Approximation) When the credit is bigger than your income tax, the cash paid out is capped at the social insurance you paid on your work: employee health insurance (including long-term care) and employee pension if you are in company insurance; otherwise National Health Insurance and National Pension. Daiwa doesn't say whether the child and childcare support levy and employment insurance also count, so we leave them out. National Health Insurance is paid by the head of the household, so it counts only toward the head's cap, and only if the head works.
- (Approximation) When the higher earner in a couple pays the spouse's National Pension premiums and deducts them, they still count toward the spouse's own cash cap. Daiwa doesn't cover this case.

All-household numbers:

- (Approximation) The survey doesn't publish data on individual households, only averages for each type of household in each ¥500,000 income range. So for each type-and-income group we make up two or three households that match the group's averages. Differences between households in the same group are lost.
- (Approximation) Each group is split into households living on pensions and households living on work, in the same ratio as pensions to total income in that group. For example, if 60% of the group's income is pensions, 60% of its households are treated as living on pensions. Households with both a pension and a wage, such as older people who still work, don't appear.
- (Approximation) The survey gives the number of people working in each household. After the heads of households living on work, the rest are spread evenly over the spouses in those households and over other adults. For example, if the share is one half, the spouse works in half of the households living on work and doesn't in the other half (spouses in households living on pensions don't work). Earnings of those who work are set so that the survey's averages stay the same.
- (Approximation) Everyone living on a pension is treated as age 70. The separate health insurance system that people aged 75 and over join (後期高齢者医療制度) is not included.
- (Approximation) All children under 18 are treated as age 10. Working adults get an age based on the survey's average for their group (between 20 and 64).
- (Approximation) Children aged 18 and over are treated as age 22. Those who work share the survey's "other family members' earnings" equally.
- (Approximation) Everyone with a wage is treated as a non-student working 20+ hours a week at a company with more than 50 employees. So people earning ¥88,000 a month or more are in the company's health insurance and pension all year, and people earning less are not. (The ¥88,000 condition ended on 1 October 2026, but from October the only people on 20+ hours paid less are workers with a minimum-wage exception, who join only if they ask.) Everyone with a wage pays employment insurance.
- (Approximation) Only wages, business income and pensions are counted. Rent, interest, dividends and money from family are left out. Business income is treated as profit after costs.
- (Approximation) Food spending is the average for households of the same kind (one person, or two or more people) with the same income, from a survey taken in October–November 2024. It isn't adjusted for the time of year.
- (Approximation) Every family member aged 70 or over other than the head and spouse is treated as a parent or grandparent of the head or spouse living with them, for the tax deduction for dependants (this deduction is larger than for other relatives aged 70 or over). Relatives who aren't parents or grandparents, such as brothers and sisters, are treated the same way. In households living on pensions, family members of the same age as the head (70) are also treated as parents.
- (Approximation) Household numbers and incomes are from 2024 and haven't been updated to 2026.
