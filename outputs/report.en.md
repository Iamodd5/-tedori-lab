# How tax and benefit plans would change take-home pay: Tedori Lab report (November 2026 edition)

## Main points

Tedori Lab works out how 4 plans to change tax and benefits would alter households' take-home pay, using the same households, data and method for each. Take-home pay means what is left after tax and insurance premiums, plus child allowance and the single-parent allowance. The calculations cover example households and every household in Japan. Only the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules) can be calculated so far.

- **Under today's rules (the 2026 rules), a household takes home ¥258,226 less a year when a part-time spouse earns ¥1,300,000 a year instead of ¥1,290,000.** This is before any of the plans. The spouse works under 20 hours a week and is not in their employer's health insurance. A point like this, where earning more leaves a household with less, is called an income wall (年収の壁). The figures are for example household H4: a couple with no children in Shinjuku, Tokyo, in which the other partner is an employee on ¥5,000,000 a year.
- (Approximation) Under the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules), 2 of the 8 example households take home more, 4 take home less and 2 see no change. The largest gain, ¥35,700 a year, is for H1 (single part-time employee (¥1.25m a year)); the largest loss, ¥157,300 a year, is for H8 (high-income dual-earner couple, one child (5)).
- (Approximation) Under the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules), the government takes in about ¥1.1 trillion more a year across the country, in a rough estimate that assumes nobody changes how they work. Income tax rises by about ¥1.5 trillion a year, and the government pays out about ¥0.4 trillion in cash: the part of the tax credit (an amount taken straight off income tax) that is bigger than the income tax owed. Daiwa's own estimate comes to about zero overall. The section on every household in Japan explains the difference.

## How to read the numbers

- Each plan is compared with '2026 rules': the rules actually in force in 2026, before any of these plans. This report also calls them today's rules.
- We assume nobody changes how much they work.
- A figure marked 'Approximation' rests on a point where the calculation had to be simplified. These points are listed at the end, under All approximations.
- Every number is produced automatically from the same calculation results as the website.
- This report does not support any plan, party or politician.

## The 4 plans compared

The government, a trade union confederation and a private research institute have put forward plans that change how working people's tax, social insurance and benefits work. Each plan states its own aims, such as easing the burden on people on low or middle incomes. Under the one plan that can be calculated now, take-home pay goes up for some households and down for others.

One form these plans take is a 'refundable tax credit' (給付付き税額控除): it cuts your income tax, and if the cut is bigger than the tax you owe, you get the rest in cash. In the government's plan, municipalities pay cash according to income, and the government calls it a "first step" towards a full refundable tax credit. The plans are:

- **Government plan (FY2027–28: 1% tax on food + payment)**: From April 2027 to March 2029, consumption tax on food and drink that is now taxed at the reduced 8% rate falls to 1%. Over the same period, people in work on low and middle incomes get a payment based on their income (the worker burden-relief payment). The government calls this two-year arrangement a bridge to the full introduction in fiscal 2029. To get the payment, earned income must be above a set level and total income at or below a set level. The tax and social insurance you pay, minus any public pension you receive, must also be above a set level. Under the bill, the payment amounts and the levels used in the conditions will be set later by Cabinet order.
- **Government plan (from FY2029)**: People in work on low and middle incomes get the worker burden-relief payment each fiscal year. This is its form from fiscal 2029, which the government calls the full introduction. The amount is based on the previous year's income and the number of dependent relatives under 19 living in Japan. To get the payment, earned income must be above a set level and total income at or below a set level. The tax and social insurance you pay, minus any public pension you receive, must also be above a set level. If you have a spouse, the spouse's total income must be at or below a set level. Under the bill, the payment amounts and the levels used in the conditions will be set later by Cabinet order.
- **Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules)**: For every taxpayer, this plan removes the income tax basic deduction (an amount taken off income before tax is worked out). In its place it gives a fixed tax credit, an amount taken straight off income tax. If the credit is bigger than your income tax, the difference is paid to you in cash. You can get up to the amount of social insurance you paid on income from work.
- **Rengo (trade union confederation) plan**: The plan has two schemes. Under one scheme, low-income employees have half of the social and employment insurance they pay taken off their income tax. Any part that can't be taken off is refunded. Under the other, people get a fixed refund for the consumption tax on minimum basic spending such as food, drink and utilities. It goes mainly to people with incomes too low to pay income tax. Rengo says it would abolish the reduced consumption tax rate when this refund starts. Rengo's documents don't give the amounts for the second scheme.

The other 3 plans cannot be calculated yet, for these reasons:

- Government plan (FY2027–28: 1% tax on food + payment): the payment amounts and income limits haven't been set out yet (under the bill, they will be set later by Cabinet order); we don't yet have food spending figures for the example households H1–H8.
- Government plan (from FY2029): the payment amounts and income limits haven't been set out yet (under the bill, they will be set later by Cabinet order).
- Rengo (trade union confederation) plan: the payment amounts and income limits for the consumption tax refund haven't been set out yet; we don't yet have food spending figures for the example households H1–H8.

## The income wall for a part-time spouse

The household in the first main point hits this wall because the spouse stops being a dependant. A dependant here is someone covered as a dependant by an employee partner's health insurance, who also pays no National Pension premiums of their own. Once that ends, National Pension (¥215,040 a year) and National Health Insurance (¥101,786 a year) premiums must be paid. Income tax and resident tax fall by ¥48,600 in all. Take-home pay gets back to where it was when the spouse earns ¥1,640,000. Pay is worked out in ¥10,000 steps.

(Approximation) Under the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules), the one plan that can be calculated now, take-home pay at the same point falls by ¥217,926 a year (¥258,226 under 2026 rules). It gets back to the same level at ¥1,600,000, again worked out in ¥10,000 steps.

Rengo's plan cannot be calculated for this household yet either (the reasons are under 'The 4 plans compared'). The government plans' effect on this household cannot be calculated, for these reasons:

- In the government's plans, people facing an income wall (年収の壁), and people with a similar social insurance burden, get a top-up (就業促進特例加算). The government's outline, the document that sets out the framework of the scheme, calls it a temporary measure until the income walls are removed.
- The outline defines the wall to include the extra premiums of moving into National Health Insurance and National Pension, not only joining company insurance. It also gives an example of a spouse leaving dependant status, as H4's spouse does.
- On the top-up's amount, the outline says it will be set considering the overall balance, among other things (「総合的なバランス等を勘案して定める」).
- From fiscal 2029, married people whose spouse's income is above a set level do not qualify for the payment. Under the bill, that level will be set later by Cabinet order. The government's basic policy calls this a limited exception for people with a high-income spouse.

These further points apply to this calculation:

- (Approximation) In a couple, we assume the partner with the higher income pays the spouse's National Pension premiums and deducts them from their own income. By law either partner may.
- From fiscal 2026, whether someone can be a dependant is judged from the yearly pay expected under their work contract. Going over the line now and then, for example through overtime that isn't in the contract, may not end dependant status. Here we work out what happens when the contracted pay goes up.
- A spouse who works at least 20 hours a week, at a company with at least 51 employees, joins that company's insurance and so does not face this wall. The all-household estimate assumes every wage earner works like that.

In the table, + means take-home pay goes up and − means it goes down.

Daiwa wrote its plan for 2025 tax rules and set the credit at ¥56,500, so that an employee could earn up to ¥1.78m a year before paying income tax. Using the same formula with 2026 tax rules gives a credit of ¥52,000. Under 2026 rules an employee can already earn ¥1.78m a year before paying income tax, even without this plan. Because of these differences, the results here differ from the numbers Daiwa published. Under 2026 rules the basic deduction that is removed is larger, and the credit is smaller, than under the rules Daiwa used. Both push take-home pay below Daiwa's figures.

## Results for every household in Japan (rough estimate)

We worked out the change in take-home pay under the Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules) for every household in Japan, by income group. The groups are ten equal-sized sets of households, lined up from lowest to highest yearly income. (Approximation) All of the following are rough estimates.

- (Approximation) In groups 1–4, take-home pay goes up on average. The biggest average gain is in group 1, at ¥9,257 a year per household. But within these groups, take-home pay goes up for 13–21% of households, and 79–85% see no change. In group 4, 7% of households are worse off.
- (Approximation) In groups 5–10, take-home pay goes down on average. The biggest average fall is in group 10, at ¥101,574 a year per household. But within these groups, take-home pay goes down for 46–98% of households and up for 2–25%, and 1–39% see no change.

The national total is in the main points. Daiwa's own estimate is about ¥0.7 trillion more income tax and about ¥0.7 trillion paid in cash, which comes to about zero overall. This is called fiscally neutral. The two estimates cannot be compared directly, because Daiwa used 2025 tax rules and a ¥56,500 credit, and this site uses 2026 rules and ¥52,000.

## Assumptions and limits

- We assume nobody changes how much they work. Some people would change their hours when the rules change; that is not included.
- The example households live in Shinjuku, Tokyo. Outside Shinjuku, the National Health Insurance and long-term care premiums and the parts of resident tax that each municipality sets are different. Outside Tokyo, so is the health insurance rate for employees in the Japan Health Insurance Association (協会けんぽ), which sets it by prefecture.
- The all-household numbers are rough estimates from made-up households matched to the averages in a 2024 government survey. They differ from official figures in these ways:
  - The household count is 2.8% lower than the official figure.
  - Total income tax is 21.5% lower and total resident tax 5.0% lower than the tax that survey itself estimates from reported incomes. This is not a comparison with tax actually collected.
  - Income tax is lower because of recent tax cuts (a larger basic deduction), too few high earners in the estimate and taxing households' average incomes, among other things. We have not measured each of these separately.
  - The survey's tax estimate is after the one-off 2024 flat tax cut, so against a normal year the shortfall would be larger (see the Checks page).
- Compared with official counts, the estimate has too many low-paid workers and too few high earners. The first makes the cash paid out look larger and the second makes the extra income tax look smaller, so the net gain to the government is more likely above this estimate's figure than below.
- Changing how the made-up households are built can move the national totals a lot, especially the cash paid out.
- The plans that cannot be calculated yet will be calculated once the amounts they need are decided and published. The reasons for each are listed under the plans above.

## All approximations

These are the points where the calculation behind a figure marked 'Approximation' was simplified.

### Behind every number

- (Approximation) Each child's age stays the same for the whole year.
- (Approximation) The single-parent allowance (児童扶養手当) uses the amounts from April 2026 for all 12 months, judged on the same year's income (in fact, the previous year's income sets the amount from November to the next October). We assume the mother receives it and gets no child support payments.
- (Approximation) In a couple, the partner with the higher income claims the deductions for the spouse and children, and pays and deducts the other partner's National Pension contributions. By law either partner may do this.
- (Approximation) Long-term care premiums for people aged 65 and over use Shinjuku's fiscal 2026 table, applied to the same year's income and resident-tax status (in fact, the previous year's decide the band). The national one-year rule for fiscal 2026 is not included. Under that rule, salary income is worked out with the old employment-income deduction, and people who stop paying resident tax only because of the new deduction are treated as paying it (介護保険法施行令, supplementary articles 24 and 25).
- (Approximation) We round the employee's share of health insurance and of the child and childcare support levy separately to the yen. A government Q&A (February 2026) said to round them once, together, but it is no longer published. The difference is at most ¥1 a month.
- (Approximation) From October 2026, the only people working 20+ hours a week for under ¥88,000 a month are workers with a minimum-wage exception, who may be paid under the minimum wage (at every regional minimum wage, 20 hours a week pays more than ¥88,000 a month). They join company insurance only if they ask; we assume they don't.
- (Approximation) Whether a student may put off National Pension payments (the student deferral) is judged on the same year's income (in fact, the previous year's).
- (Approximation) Health and long-term care insurance rates change from March 2026 and employment insurance from April; the child and childcare support levy is new from April (the employee pension rate, 18.3%, does not change). We use the new rates and the levy for all 12 months of 2026. National Pension uses the fiscal 2026 monthly amount for every month. National Health Insurance uses Shinjuku's fiscal 2026 rates, because the fiscal 2027 rates (which would apply to 2026 income) aren't published yet. Pay is the same every month, with no bonus.

### Daiwa Institute plan (adjusted to 2026 tax rules)

- (Approximation) Income tax has a 2.1% reconstruction surtax added on top. Daiwa doesn't say whether the credit is subtracted before or after that surtax. We subtract the credit first and then add the 2.1%, which is how existing tax credits work.
- (Approximation) When the credit is bigger than your income tax, the cash paid out is capped at the social insurance you paid on your work. If you are in company insurance, that means employee health insurance (including long-term care) and employee pension. Otherwise it means National Health Insurance and National Pension. For people aged 65 or over who work, the long-term care premium counts too. Daiwa doesn't say whether the child and childcare support levy and employment insurance also count, so we leave them out. National Health Insurance premiums are owed by the head of the household, so here they count only toward the head's cap, and only if the head works.
- (Approximation) When the higher earner in a couple pays the spouse's National Pension premiums and deducts them, they still count toward the spouse's own cash cap. Daiwa doesn't cover this case.

### All-household numbers

- (Approximation) The survey doesn't publish data on individual households, only averages for each type of household in each ¥500,000 income range. So for each combination of type and income we make up one to six households (about four on average, about 1,200 in all) that match its averages. Differences between households in the same combination are lost.
- (Approximation) Each combination is split into households living on pensions and households living on work, in the same ratio as pensions to total income in that combination. For example, if 60% of its income is pensions, 60% of its households are treated as living on pensions. Households with both a pension and a wage, such as older people who still work, don't appear.
- (Approximation) The survey gives the number of people working in each household. After the heads of households living on work, the rest are spread evenly over the spouses in those households and over other adults. For example, if the share is one half, the spouse works in half of the households living on work and doesn't in the other half (spouses in households living on pensions don't work). Earnings of those who work are set so that the survey's averages stay the same.
- (Approximation) Everyone living on a pension is treated as age 70. The separate health insurance system that people aged 75 and over join (後期高齢者医療制度) is not included.
- (Approximation) All children under 18 are treated as age 10. Working adults get an age based on the survey's average for their group (between 20 and 64).
- (Approximation) Children aged 18 and over are treated as age 22. Those who work share the survey's "other family members' earnings" equally.
- (Approximation) Everyone with a wage is treated as a non-student working 20+ hours a week at a company with at least 51 employees. So people earning ¥88,000 a month or more are in the company's health insurance and pension all year, and people earning less are not. (The ¥88,000 condition ended on 1 October 2026, but from October the only people on 20+ hours paid less than ¥88,000 a month are workers with a minimum-wage exception, who join only if they ask.) Everyone with a wage pays employment insurance.
- (Approximation) Only wages, business income and pensions are counted. Rent, interest, dividends and money from family are left out. Business income is treated as profit after costs.
- (Approximation) Food spending is the average for households of the same kind (one person, or two or more people) with the same income, from a survey taken in October–November 2024. It isn't adjusted for the time of year.
- (Approximation) Every family member aged 70 or over other than the head and spouse is treated as a parent or grandparent of the head or spouse living with them, for the tax deduction for dependants (this deduction is larger than for other relatives aged 70 or over). Relatives who aren't parents or grandparents, such as brothers and sisters, are treated the same way. In households living on pensions, family members of the same age as the head (70) are also treated as parents.
- (Approximation) Household numbers and incomes are from 2024 and haven't been updated to 2026.
